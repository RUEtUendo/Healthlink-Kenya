April, 2026Abstract
Kenya’s public health system allocates resources reactively rather than in anticipation of
demand, creating preventable access gaps among geographically remote and economically
marginalised populations. This study developedHealthLink Kenya, a modular, two-stage
predictive framework grounded in the Andersen Behavioural Model, using nationally rep-
resentative microdata from the 2022 Kenya Integrated Household Health and Economic
Survey (KNBS; n=99,031 ; analytical n=73,059 ). A nine-algorithm tournament iden-
tified XGBoost as the optimal classifier for initial care access (Stage 1: AUC =0.8144 ,
F1=0.8091 ) and follow-up retention (Stage 2: AUC =0.8510 ,F1=0.9836 ), with bootstrap
validation ( n=1,000 iterations) confirming stability across both stages. TreeSHAP analysis
identified predisposing factors as the dominant predictors ( 73.2% of model weight), while
distance_from_facility was the most influential individual feature ( 12.9% ), reflecting
perceived rather than verified geographic proximity a material limitation of the study’s spatial
conclusions. A severe 28.74 :1class imbalance constrained the baseline retention model to
near-zero dropout recall ( 5%); SMOTE oversampling raised recall to 53.4% (precision 0.208 ,
AUC 0.829 ), representing the recommended production configuration. The framework was
translated into theHealthLink KenyaStreamlit dashboard, a dual-profile decision-support
tool. Social workers access a geospatial and predictive triage view to identify health-evasive
households; patients access a simplified personal interface for facility navigation and plain-
language retention guidance. Analytical validation is complete; operational adoption requires
structured field deployment, institutional mandate, and a pre-deployment ethical governance
audit before county-level rollout.
Keywords:health-seeking behaviour, XGBoost, SHAP interpretability, Andersen Be-
havioural Model, healthcare access, referral planning, Kenya, predictive modelling, data-
driven decision support.

x1 Introduction
1.1 Background to the Study
Healthcare accessibility remains a major global challenge, particularly in low- and middle-
income countries (LMICs) where uneven facility distribution, transportation barriers, and
limited health system coordination delay treatment and reduce service utilisation. In Kenya,
this challenge is compounded by the persistent fragmentation of health data systems, which
constrains the ability of planners and administrators to anticipate demand, coordinate refer-
rals, and allocate resources efficiently. Most studies on health-seeking behaviour (HSB) in
Kenya have remained largely descriptive and operationally isolated (Mwaura et al., 2025),
limiting their integration across health departments and administrative levels. Existing digital
tools for analysing HSB are often rigid, proprietary, and unsuitable for low-connectivity
environments, making them difficult to scale in resource-limited settings. A further criti-
cal absence exists in interoperable frameworks that can connect behavioural insights with
geospatial, clinical, and administrative data (Musiega et al., 2023).
This study addressed this gap by developingHealthLink Kenya, a modular, two-stage
predictive framework that harmonises health-seeking behaviour data from the 2022 Kenya
Integrated Household Health and Economic Survey (KNBS) and translates analytical out-
puts into a practical decision-support dashboard for facility-level referral planning. The
framework was grounded in the Andersen Behavioural Model, which organises determinants
of healthcare utilisation into predisposing, enabling, and need factors, providing a theoret-
ically coherent structure for both feature engineering and result interpretation. Crucially,
the framework was designed around two underserved actors: patients who navigate care
pathways under geographic and informational constraints, and social workers who conduct
community outreach without access to data-driven prioritisation tools.
11.1.1 Big Data Considerations
The rapid growth of big data and artificial intelligence (AI) in healthcare introduces both
opportunities and risks for public health systems in LMICs. In Kenya, challenges including
poor data quality, privacy concerns, and weak enforcement of data protection legislation
risk undermining public trust in digital health tools, despite the presence of formal legal
protections (Masinde et al., 2025). These conditions underscore the need for research
methods that are scalable, context-sensitive, and embedded with strong data governance
safeguards.
The value of flexible, modular design has been demonstrated across multiple Kenyan
digital health contexts. A modular HIV PrEP training programme improved provider
knowledge while accommodating scheduling constraints in busy clinical settings (Irungu
et al., 2022). Similarly, blended training programmes for midwives in Kenya and Nigeria
enhanced learning outcomes despite persistent gaps in digital literacy and internet reliability
(Shikuku et al., 2024). A stakeholder-led framework for neglected tropical diseases further
demonstrated that tools remain practical and sustainable when guidelines are developed with
end-user input and updated regularly (Ng’etich et al., 2021).
Collectively, these precedents establish that frameworks designed for diverse, resource-
limited populations must leverage affordable and flexible digital tools (Odeny et al., 2020),
embed ethical and context-aware analytics, and maintain interpretability for non-technical
end-users. These principles directly informed the design choices made in this study, par-
ticularly the emphasis on open-source tooling, modular preprocessing, and SHAP-based
interpretability.
1.2 Problem Statement
Despite the widespread adoption of digital health systems in Kenya, healthcare data remain
fragmented, underutilised, and poorly integrated across administrative levels. Existing plat-
forms prioritise clinical record management and aggregate reporting rather than operational
decision support, with the result that referral decisions are frequently driven by incomplete
2information, informal networks, or localised knowledge rather than integrated analytical
tools (Musiega et al., 2023). This fragmentation limits efficient resource allocation, creates
barriers to timely treatment, and delays care for the populations most in need.
Two categories of user are left structurally unsupported by current systems. Patients
navigating Kenya’s public health system make access decisions under uncertainty, often
without knowledge of which facilities are geographically reachable, NHIF-accredited, or
appropriate for their needs. Social workers and Community Health V olunteers, tasked with
reaching health-evasive households across peri-urban and rural coverage areas, operate
without data-driven tools to identify or prioritise at-risk individuals. Outreach in this
environment is necessarily reactive rather than predictive.
These challenges are compounded by rigid system designs that overlook regional dis-
parities in population distribution, facility capacity, and transportation barriers (Mwaura
et al., 2025), and by infrastructural constraints including unstable connectivity and low data
privacy awareness that complicate the ethical use of digital health tools (Masinde et al.,
2025).
To address this gap, this study developed a data-driven analytical framework integrating
geographic, facility-level, and behavioural data into a modular prediction pipeline and dual-
profile decision-support dashboard serving social workers through predictive risk triage and
geospatial outreach mapping, and patients through personalised care pathway guidance, both
grounded in empirically validated predictive models.
1.3 Research Objectives
The primary objective of this study was to develop, implement, and evaluate a modular,
interpretable data-driven framework for health-seeking behaviour analytics, assessing its
predictive accuracy, interpretability, and operational utility for supporting evidence-based
referral planning and decision-making within Kenya’s public health system.
31.3.1 Specific Objectives
•Objective 1:To evaluate the key structural, geographic, and socioeconomic determi-
nants of health-seeking behaviour in LMICs and Kenya, identifying the limitations of
traditional analytical approaches.
•Objective 2:To conduct a detailed analysis of machine learning and explainable AI
(XAI) studies in healthcare, evaluating their methodological approaches, weaknesses,
and capacity to address class imbalance.
•Objective 3:To design and implement a reproducible, Python-based data pipeline
capable of harmonising heterogeneous datasets in strict alignment with the Andersen
Behavioural Model.
•Objective 4:To develop, evaluate, and operationalise a two-stage supervised machine
learning framework into an interactive decision-support dashboard (HealthLink Kenya),
directly supporting facility-level referral planning and community outreach.
1.4 Research Questions
Research Question 1:
What structural, geographic, and socioeconomic factors most strongly determine health-
seeking behaviour and provider selection within Kenya’s health system, and how can
interpretable models quantify their relative influence?
Research Question 2:
How can heterogeneous health-seeking behaviour datasets be harmonised into a modular,
reproducible, and interpretable analytical pipeline aligned with the Andersen Behavioural
Model?
Research Question 3:
To what extent can a two-stage supervised machine learning framework accurately predict
initial care access and follow-up care retention among Kenyan households, and how ef-
4fectively does the resulting decision-support system enhance evidence-based planning and
resource allocation within Kenya’s public health system?
1.5 Research Gap
Existing studies on healthcare accessibility and health-seeking behaviour have produced
valuable insights into the socioeconomic and geographic factors influencing healthcare utili-
sation. Concurrently, digital health platforms such as electronic medical record systems and
national health information infrastructures have improved data collection and management.
However, these systems have largely focused on clinical documentation, aggregate reporting,
or high-level policy monitoring, rather than operational decision support at the facility level
(Musiega et al., 2023).
While interoperability standards such as DHIS2 integrations and FHIR-based data
exchange facilitate health information sharing, they do not inherently provide analytical
frameworks that transform fragmented accessibility data into actionable tools for healthcare
providers. Existing architectures primarily address data exchange and reporting infrastruc-
ture; this study focused instead on the analytical transformation of health-seeking behaviour
datasets into operational referral decision-support tools. In particular, limited prior research
has translated heterogeneous accessibility datasets into systems capable of assisting both
patients and social workers based on geographic accessibility and behavioural risk.
As a consequence, hospitals in many LMICs continue to rely on informal knowledge or
incomplete information when determining appropriate referral destinations. This highlights
a persistent gap between the availability of healthcare data and the development of practical
analytical tools that support referral planning and operational coordination. This study
addressed that gap by developing a modular data pipeline and interpretable decision -support
dashboard grounded in nationally representative survey data and validated machine learning
models.(Moucheraud et al., 2022)
51.6 Research Justification
The proposed framework was grounded in principles of modularity, adaptability, and in-
terpretability to address the operational challenges associated with fragmented healthcare
accessibility data. Many digital health systems in LMICs remain difficult to scale or adapt
due to rigid architectures and limited analytical integration. Modular data architectures
offer a practical alternative by enabling incremental development, local customisation, and
reusable analytical components that can be adapted across different health system contexts
(Odeny et al., 2020).
To support this approach, the study implemented a Python-based end-to-end data pipeline
that harmonised the four feature modules of the Andersen Behavioural Model health utilisa-
tion behaviour, predisposing socio-demographic factors, enabling socio-economic factors,
and geographic access factors into a reproducible analytical structure. By standardising data
preparation, transformation, and modelling processes, the pipeline promoted transparency,
repeatability, and extensibility across health system contexts.
The analytical layer of the framework emphasised methods that balance interpretability
with predictive capability. XGBoost was selected as the optimal classifier following a
nine-algorithm tournament evaluated on ROC-AUC, given the class-imbalanced nature of
the dataset. TreeSHAP visualisations ensured that model outputs remained transparent and
understandable for health administrators and policymakers who rely on these insights for
operational planning.
The framework’s operational utility, however, is contingent on more than technical
performance. Social workers and Community Health V olunteers in Kenya’s public health
system operate under significant structural constraints high caseloads, limited digital literacy,
unpredictable connectivity, and in many cases voluntary or low-compensated roles with
high turnover rates (Njiraini and Hussein, 2020). A dashboard that is analytically sound but
requires substantial training, uninterrupted data access, or significant time investment per
case risks becoming operationally unused regardless of its predictive performance.

1.7 Research Scope
This study focused on developing an analytical framework for evaluating healthcare accessi-
bility and supporting referral planning within Kenya’s healthcare system. The framework
integrated secondary data from the KNBS 2022 Health-Seeking Behaviour Survey together
with facility-level information to analyse structural factors influencing healthcare accessibil-
ity.
The analysis examined key determinants including education level, wealth index, distance
to care, insurance status, and residential area, as well as provider categories such as clinics,
county hospitals, national referral hospitals, and traditional healers. These variables were
organised into the four modules of the Andersen Behavioural Model and used to construct
analytical indicators informing the identification of accessible healthcare providers and
potential referral options.
The study was bounded by several scope constraints. The analysis relied on secondary sur-
vey data rather than clinical medical records, and the dataset did not include high-resolution
7spatial coordinates that would enable detailed geospatial modelling at the individual facility
level. The framework focused on accessibility and referral support rather than clinical
diagnosis prediction or real-time hospital capacity monitoring. The retention model (Stage 2)
was further constrained by a severe class imbalance (96.2% retained), which limited its
utility for identifying true dropout cases despite a strong overall AUC.
1.8 Research Limitations
Generalisability and Local Context.Although the framework was designed for broad
applicability, local cultural, administrative, and infrastructural factors require contextual
adaptation for deployment in specific regions. The absence of highly granular sub-county
variables may have constrained predictive accuracy for isolated sub-populations.
Data Availability and Quality.The dataset’s heavy skew towards positive healthcare
utilisation (82% visited, 96% retained) introduced a sensitivity bias in both models, priori-
tising the identification of access over exclusion. Missing and inconsistent values required
median imputation for continuous variables and mode imputation for categorical variables.
Additionally, the imputation of missing target variable values ( visited_provider and
revisited_provider ) as positive responses introduces a conservative analytical assump-
tion that is acknowledged as a limitation of the study design.
Absence of High-Resolution Spatial Coordinates.The KNBS 2022 dataset provides
self-reported estimates of distance to the nearest facility rather than GPS-derived geodesic
measurements. This distinction is analytically consequential: self-reported distance is subject
to respondent perception bias, does not account for road network topology or seasonal
accessibility, and cannot support travel-time catchment modelling at the sub-county level.
All spatial analysis in this study is therefore bounded by the precision of survey-reported
distance, and the distance-decay thresholds identified by the GAM should be interpreted as
approximations rather than precise geometric boundaries.
This limitation was empirically validated as part of the study’s future work scaffold. A
Nominatim-based geocoding pipeline was applied to the 10,668 unique facility names in the
8KNBS dataset, successfully geocoding 1,558 facilities ( 14.6% ). Geodesic distances were
then computed from each geocoded facility’s verified GPS coordinates to the centroid of its
inferred county, producing a geodesic distance estimate for 23,259 analytical rows ( 23.5%
coverage). The Pearson correlation between self-reported and geodesic distance across
these matched rows was r=−0.040 a near-zero coefficient indicating that respondents’
perceptions of distance bear no systematic linear relationship to verified geographic distance.
This finding constitutes a material limitation of the study’s spatial conclusions: the GAM-
derived 35 km threshold and the SHAP distance attribution should be interpreted as reflecting
respondent perceptions, not verified geographic access constraints. The result empirically
substantiates the theoretical concern about perception bias and suggests that replacing self-
reported distance with GPS-derived geodesic measurements would introduce materially
different and more analytically valid spatial signal into the model.
Cross-Sectional Design and Absence of Longitudinal Linkage.The KNBS 2022
survey is a single cross-sectional instrument. Individual respondents cannot be linked across
survey waves, preventing the construction of care-seeking trajectories over time. As a result,
the retention model captures a static snapshot of revisit behaviour rather than a dynamic
view of long-term engagement with the health system. Patients who cycle in and out of
care, switch providers between survey rounds, or whose access barriers change with life
circumstances are analytically invisible in this design. Individual-level linkage across waves
would require either a panel survey component or probabilistic record linkage to national
health information registries, both of which fall outside the scope of secondary data analysis
(Masinde et al., 2025).
Privacy and Ethical Constraints.The use of anonymised secondary data protected
participant confidentiality but resulted in the loss of contextual depth that primary data
collection would have provided. Facility-level quality variables including medicine stockout
rates, clinician-to-patient ratios, and patient-reported waiting times are absent from the
KNBS instrument, constraining the model’s ability to distinguish geographic access failure
from quality-of-care failure at the prediction stage rather than retrospectively through error
analysis.
9Practical and Technical Constraints.The modular pipeline requires Python-based
computational infrastructure and technical expertise to execute and maintain. Long-term
adoption will depend on robust documentation, local capacity-building, and institutional
support for sustainable deployment.
Interpretability versus Complexity.While interpretable models promoted transparency
and stakeholder trust, ensemble methods such as XGBoost may compress predictive precision
relative to deep learning alternatives. Even with TreeSHAP visualisations, unmeasured or
context-specific variables for example facility opening hours, seasonal road access, or
household illness severity may remain undetected by the model.
These limitations underscore the importance of local adaptation, transparent documenta-
tion, and realistic expectations regarding model accuracy in operational deployment. Future
iterations of this pipeline should expand regional customisation, improve data harmonisation
techniques, and strengthen capacity-building for locally managed use.

2.1 Introduction
This chapter critically examines the theoretical, methodological, and operational literature
relevant to healthcare accessibility analytics in low- and middle-income country (LMIC) con-
texts. The review is organised around five interconnected themes, each of which contributes
to the analytical and design rationale of theHealthLink Kenyaframework.
Three substantive gaps motivate the study and run as a thread through each section.
First, Andersen’s Behavioural Model (Li et al., 2023; Solanke et al., 2023b) the dominant
conceptual framework for healthcare utilisation research is widely applied descriptively in
LMIC settings but rarely translated as a structured predictive feature engineering architecture.
Second, physical distance to care is routinely modelled as a linear predictor, suppressing
nonlinear threshold effects that carry direct policy consequences (Waqas et al., 2024; Belay
et al., 2025). Third, despite demonstrable advances in machine learning and explainable
artificial intelligence (XAI), the deployment of interpretable predictive models within African
health information systems remains practically underdeveloped (Chepkirui et al., 2025;
Masinde et al., 2025).
Woven across all three gaps is a consistent finding from the health-seeking behaviour
literature: the populations most at risk of exclusion from formal healthcare health-evasive
individuals and under-resourced social outreach workers are also the populations least
served by existing digital health tools. TheHealthLink Kenyaframework responds directly
to this dual deficit, designing separate but analytically unified interfaces for the patient
(P) navigating care under constraint, and the social worker (SW) conducting community
outreach without data-driven prioritisation support.

Model and the case for its computational operationalisation. Section 2.3 examines the
distance-decay literature and the methodological limitations of linear spatial modelling.

framework.
2.2 Theoretical Foundation: Andersen’s Behavioural Model
2.2.1 Origins and Core Structure
Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Services Use, first proposed in the 1960s and sub-
sequently refined (Andersen, 2024; Li et al., 2023), organises the determinants of healthcare
utilisation into three analytically distinct categories:predisposing characteristicsdemo-
graphic and social attributes that precede illness, including age, gender, and education
level;enabling resourcesstructural conditions that facilitate or impede access, including
income, insurance coverage, and proximity to facilities; andperceived or evaluated need
subjective and clinical illness assessments that motivate care-seeking. The model’s value
lies in providing a taxonomically coherent classification system that enables comparative
research on healthcare disparities across diverse institutional and geographic contexts.
The model has been applied across a wide range of LMIC health contexts. Orhan and
Kurutkan (2025) applied Andersen’s three factor categories directly to a machine learning
pipeline for predicting total healthcare demand, demonstrating that the taxonomy can serve
as both a descriptive organiser and a feature engineering scaffold. Kassaw et al. (2025)
similarly operationalised the model to predict healthcare access among rural pregnant
women in Ethiopia, confirming that predisposing and enabling factors jointly dominate
access outcomes in resource-limited settings a finding that directly anticipates theAndersen
Reversaldocumented in this study’s SHAP analysis.
13Figure 2.1:Andersen’s Behavioural Model: policy application framework illustrating the
hierarchical relationship between predisposing, enabling, and need factors as determinants
of healthcare utilisation.
2.2.2 Limitations in LMIC Contexts
Andersen’s original formulation was constructed within high-income healthcare systems
characterised by stable institutional infrastructures, consistent data standards, and limited
geographic heterogeneity. Three structural limitations constrain its direct application in
LMIC contexts.
First, the model treats enabling factors as static attributes, whereas in environments such
as Kenya, insurance coverage, facility availability, and transport access vary substantially
across seasons, administrative boundaries, and policy cycles (Nyangena et al., 2021; Chep-
kirui et al., 2025). Second, fragmented digital health infrastructures and inconsistent coding
standards prevent the integration of behavioural, spatial, and administrative datasets into
unified analytical structures (Masinde et al., 2025), rendering the model descriptive rather
than practically applicable. Third, empirical applications in sub-Saharan African contexts
consistently find that enabling factors particularly geographic distance and economic en-
abling conditions dominate utilisation decisions to a degree that the original formulation
does not adequately weight (Solanke et al., 2023a; Orhan and Kurutkan, 2025).
Rather than invalidating the model, these constraints highlight the necessity for its
computational adaptation. The current study addresses this by translating Andersen’s three
14factor categories directly into four structured feature engineering modules, transforming the
framework from a descriptive taxonomy into a predictive architecture.
2.2.3 Implementation Through Machine Learning
Recent methodological developments in machine learning and explainable AI open substan-
tive pathways for modernising Andersen’s framework beyond descriptive application. SHAP
attribution methods enable the estimation of nonlinear, high-order relationships among pre-
disposing, enabling, and need-related features while preserving interpretability accessible to
non-technical policymakers (Orsini et al., 2022; Yehuala, 2024). Emerging semantics-aware
data harmonisation standards further support the integration of heterogeneous datasets from
multiple administrative and geographic domains (Dylag et al., 2024; Saras et al., 2025).
Building on these precedents, this study translates the Andersen taxonomy as a structured
feature engineering pipeline, anchoring the study’s primary methodological contribution
and providing the theoretical basis for interpreting SHAP outputs in terms of the model’s
original factor categories.
2.3 Distance and Spatial Barriers to Healthcare Access
2.3.1 The Distance-Decay Effect
Physical distance to healthcare facilities is consistently identified as the most influential
enabling factor in Andersen’s model across LMIC contexts (Masinde et al., 2025; Ng’etich
et al., 2021). Rural and geographically marginalised communities face compounding access
barriers: long travel distances are associated with inadequate transportation infrastructure,
higher indirect costs, and diminished insurance penetration, all of which independently
suppress both initial utilisation and treatment adherence (Belay et al., 2025). The spatial
distribution of health facilities in Kenya exemplifies the severity of this challenge: ur-
ban counties particularly Nairobi and Central Rift concentrate the majority of private and
specialised facilities, while arid and semi-arid counties in the north and north-east face
15acute service shortfalls, with large populations residing beyond any reasonable travel radius
(Moturi et al., 2022).

162.3.2 The Inadequacy of Linear Distance Modelling
The methodological treatment of distance as a linear predictor constitutes a systematic
error in the existing literature. Linear distance assumptions understate the complexity of
the access environment in two specific ways. First, the relationship between distance and
utilisation exhibits pronounced threshold effects: utilisation remains relatively stable up to a
critical distance, beyond which it declines sharply (Waqas et al., 2024). Imposing linearity
suppresses the identification of these thresholds and produces misspecified models that
underestimate exclusion risk for populations residing marginally beyond a tipping point.
Second, linear coefficients cannot distinguish between populations facing infrastructural
access failure (no road access) and those facing cost-based access failure (road exists but
transport is unaffordable) distinctions with materially different policy implications (Belay
et al., 2025).
This methodological limitation carries a direct consequence for thePexperience. A
patient whose estimated distance falls just below a linearly derived threshold is treated as
equivalent to one living within easy reach; a nonlinear model reveals this as a false equiva-
lence. TheHealthLink Kenyaframework addresses this by fitting a GAM to characterise
the true functional form of the distance-utilisation relationship before applying XGBoost to
capture higher-order spatial interactions.
2.3.3 Generalized Additive Models as a Methodological Advance
Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) replace fixed linear coefficients with flexible smooth
spline functions, enabling the empirical estimation of nonlinear distance-utilisation rela-
tionships and the identification of policy-relevant inflection points that linear specifications
cannot recover (Waqas et al., 2024; Belay et al., 2025). GAMs thus provide a methodologi-
cally rigorous basis for recalibrating facility placement standards, ambulance dispatch radii,
and mobile clinic deployment schedules. In this study, the GAM serves as a complement to
the XGBoost classification model: it characterises the spatial form of the distance-utilisation
17relationship and derives the 35 km threshold that is subsequently operationalised as the grid
zone boundary in the Nakuru County pilot deployment.
2.4 Machine Learning for Health-Seeking Behaviour Analytics
2.4.1 Ensemble Methods and Their Analytical Advantages
Ensemble learning methods have demonstrated systematic superiority over single-algorithm
approaches in healthcare analytics tasks, specifically because health-seeking behaviour
data exhibit high-dimensional feature interactions, nonlinear covariate relationships, and
pronounced class imbalance that regression-based methods cannot adequately model (Faye
et al., 2025; Taye et al., 2025). Random Forest algorithms address overfitting through
bootstrap aggregation across decorrelated decision trees (Avinash et al., 2025). Gradient
boosting methods including XGBoost (Wiens et al., 2025) extend this framework by sequen-
tially training learners on residual errors, achieving higher discriminative performance in
imbalanced classification tasks and demonstrating robustness on structured tabular health
survey data (Soladoye et al., 2025). Seto et al. (2022) further demonstrated that gradient
boosting consistently outperforms logistic regression in predicting probability for chronic
disease outcomes using large-scale survey data, a finding that directly informs the algorithm
tournament implemented in this study.
The nine-algorithm tournament implemented here evaluated on ROC-AUC given the
class-imbalanced target distribution provides a systematic empirical basis for algorithm
selection rather than relying on prior assumption, and generates the performance comparison
that justifies XGBoost as the foundation of both the access (Stage 1) and retention (Stage 2)
classifiers.
2.4.2 Class Imbalance as a Methodological and Policy Problem
Class imbalance is a structural property of national health survey data, not an incidental data
quality issue. In populations where the majority of respondents report having accessed care,
algorithms that minimise aggregate classification error will disproportionately optimise for
18the majority class, producing high apparent accuracy while systematically failing to identify
the minority populations most at risk of exclusion (Carvalho et al., 2025; Sani et al., 2025).
In the healthcare access context, false negatives individuals predicted to access care who
do not represent missed opportunities for targeted intervention: the very populations that
decision-support tools are intended to serve.
This distinction carries direct operational weight for bothPandSW. A patient who
is misclassified as low-risk will not appear in the social worker’s outreach queue, even if
they are a genuine dropout risk. A social worker operating on flawed triage outputs will
systematically under-reach the most marginalised households. Accordingly, this study incor-
porates the scale_pos_weight parameter in XGBoost and evaluates model performance
using F1-score and ROC-AUC rather than standard accuracy (Carvalho et al., 2025; Sani
et al., 2025). The choice between SMOTE oversampling and cost-sensitive weighting and
the implications of that choice for geographic coordinate integrity is documented in detail in
Chapter 4.
2.4.3 Explainable Artificial Intelligence and Algorithmic Accountability
High-performance ensemble models in healthcare applications face a fundamental account-
ability problem: predictions that cannot be explained cannot be acted upon responsibly by
clinicians, administrators, or policymakers (Alharthi et al., 2025; Awe et al., 2025). SHAP
(SHapley Additive exPlanations), grounded in cooperative game theory, addresses this prob-
lem by computing the marginal contribution of each input feature to a model’s prediction,
averaged across all possible feature subsets, yielding consistent, theoretically principled
attribution scores (Alharthi et al., 2025). TreeSHAP (Lundberg et al., 2020) extends this to
tree-based ensembles with polynomial-time computation, enabling global feature attribution
across the full test set and local explanation of individual predictions.
The accountability dimension of XAI is not merely technical. ForSWusing a predictive
triage tool, a SHAP output that identifies distance as the dominant driver of exclusion for one
cluster and low wealth as the dominant driver for another directly informshowto structure
the outreach whether to address transport barriers or economic ones. ForP, a plain-language
19rendering of the same SHAP output enables personalised, self-directed care navigation
without requiring statistical literacy. By applying TreeSHAP to the XGBoost access model,
this thesis quantifies the predictive weight of enabling factors distance, wealth index, and
insurance status thereby empirically validating the Andersen model hierarchy and equipping
policymakers with a prioritised account of actionable barriers (Noor et al., 2025; Kita et al.,
2025).
2.5 Health-Evasive Populations, Social Outreach, Patient Self-Navigation, and Ethical
Constraints
This section deliberately integrates four themes that are frequently treated in isolation in the
literature: the characteristics of health-evasive populations, the evidence base for community
health worker outreach, the conditions for effective patient self-navigation, and the ethical
obligations generated when predictive risk tools are deployed in community settings. These
themes are inseparable in practice: the design of a tool forPandSWcannot be decoupled
from an honest account of who these users are, what structural constraints they face, and
what harm may result from misclassification or misuse.
2.5.1 The Health-Evasive Population: Definition and Global Evidence
A structurally distinct population sits at the intersection of every major barrier documented
in the health-seeking behaviour literature: individuals who do not present to formal health
services not because care is entirely inaccessible, but because the cumulative weight of
social, cultural, economic, and experiential deterrents exceeds their perceived benefit of
engagement. This phenomenon variously described as healthcare avoidance, passive non-
utilisation, or voluntary disengagement is empirically distinguishable from access failure
caused by geographic exclusion (Kraschenin et al., 2008). Where geographic exclusion
represents a hard supply-side constraint, health-evasive behaviour represents a demand-side
failure that persists even when facilities are within reach.
20Globally, health-evasive populations are disproportionately represented among specific
demographic clusters: young adult males who associate care-seeking with vulnerability
(Gilbert et al., 2016); indigenous and marginalised ethnic communities whose historical
experiences of institutional discrimination have eroded systemic trust (Srugo et al., 2023);
undocumented or informally employed individuals who fear administrative consequences of
formal health registration (DeRose et al., 2015); and chronically poor households for whom
the indirect costs of care transport, lost wages, childcare constitute a stronger deterrent than
the direct cost of treatment itself (ASPE, 2015). What unites these groups is not the absence
of a nearby facility but the presence of a barrier that no map can measure.
In Kenya, this pattern acquires a specific geographic texture. Even in counties where
the urban proximity paradox holds where populations residing within a moderate distance
of a facility systematically fail to utilise it evasion rather than distance is often the primary
mechanism of exclusion (Noor et al., 2022). Low health literacy, distrust of biomedical
paradigms, reliance on traditional healers as first-contact providers, and gender norms
governing who may seek care without stigma all contribute to this pattern (Cheruto, 2020;
Ofori et al., 2025). The operational consequence is that analytical frameworks designed
exclusively around geographic accessibility systematically misclassify a large segment of
the at-risk population: individuals who appear accessible by distance metrics but remain
effectively excluded by behavioural and social determinants.
This characterisation carries a direct implication for the tool design. A patient who avoids
formal health systems by disposition is by definition among the least likely users of a digital
self-navigation tool requiring login, data entry, and interpretation of algorithmic outputs.
This structural paradox faces all patient-facing digital health tools targeting hard-to-reach
populations (Labrique et al., 2017; Valdivia et al., 2019). The design resolution adopted
in this study is to reframe the patient portal’s primary function: not as a tool that reverses
evasion through self-directed engagement, but as a retention and re-engagement support
tool most powerfully used during a mediated encounter initiated by a social worker or a
Community Health Volunteer (CHV)). Evasion reversal is theSWconsole’s function. The
patient portal’s function is to reduce dropout once engagement has begun.
212.5.2 Community Health Workers and Social Workers as Demand-Side Bridges
The global evidence base forCommunity Health Workers (CHWs)and social worker
programme and social worker programmes consistently demonstrates that outreach-based,
relationship-mediated models of care engagement produce measurable improvements in
utilisation among health-evasive populations where facility-based interventions have failed
(Perkins et al., 2019). CHWs operating within the communities they serve carry a form
of social capital that institutional facilities cannot replicate: familiarity, linguistic and
cultural fluency, and the trust accumulated through sustained personal presence. In sub-
Saharan African contexts, CHW programmes have demonstrated impact across maternal
and child health uptake, HIV treatment adherence, tuberculosis case-finding, and non-
communicable disease screening precisely the domains in which passive, facility-dependent
systems consistently underperform among marginalised populations (Perkins et al., 2019;
Solanke et al., 2023b).
The critical constraint on CHW and social worker effectiveness is targeting. When
outreach workers must identify at-risk or evasive households through informal community
knowledge alone, their reach is bounded by personal networks and geographic familiarity.
Systematic under-coverage of the most isolated or socially marginalised households those
least likely to be known to anyone with institutional contact is a documented limitation
of non-data-guided outreach programmes (Nelson and Madiba). ASWequipped with a
risk-stratified geospatial map of their catchment area can direct limited outreach time toward
the individuals least likely to self-present. The SHAP attribution layer further enriches this
capability: understandingwhya specific community cluster is predicted to be evasive enables
the worker to tailor their engagement approach rather than delivering a generic intervention
to a heterogeneous population.
Kenya’s community health system provides an existing institutional scaffold for this
model. The CHV network already deploys community-level workers tasked with first-contact
health promotion and referral linkage (Njiraini and Hussein, 2020). What this infrastructure
has historically lacked is a data layer: a structured, interpretable analytical tool that translates
22national survey evidence and predictive model outputs into spatially actionable outreach
intelligence.
Designing a tool for this population, however, is not equivalent to integrating it into
practice. The CHV workforce in Kenya is characterised by high turnover, limited formal
training in data interpretation, and working conditions often unpaid or incentive-based that
constrain the time available for any activity beyond direct household contact (Njiraini and
Hussein, 2020). Evidence from analogous data-driven outreach tools in sub-Saharan Africa
consistently identifies the same implementation barriers: inadequate training time, absence
of supervisory reinforcement, and the perception among frontline workers that digital tools
add administrative burden without reducing physical workload (Chepkirui et al., 2025; Lilan
et al., 2025). ForHealthLink Kenyato fulfil its intended function, deployment must be
accompanied by a structured onboarding protocol, a plain-language field guide that does
not require prior statistical literacy, supervisory-level adoption mandates from county health
management, and a feedback mechanism through which social workers can flag erroneous
predictions. Without these conditions, the tool addresses a targeting gap in principle while
the gap persists in practice.
2.5.3 Patient Self-Navigation: Evidence and Design Conditions
Parallel to the evidence for outreach-based models, a growing body of literature documents
the conditions under which patients can effectively navigate health systems when provided
with appropriately designed informational tools (Valdivia et al., 2019). Patient self-navigation
the capacity of an individual to identify accessible care options, understand eligibility
constraints, and make an informed facility selection without requiring clinical intermediation
reduces dependence on institutional gatekeeping and has been associated with improved
treatment initiation rates (Valdivia et al., 2019).
The design conditions for effective patient-facing health tools in LMIC settings are well
characterised. Interfaces must minimise cognitive load, present only information directly
relevant to the individual’s decision context, and be optimised for daytime outdoor readability
on low-cost devices with inconsistent connectivity (Labrique et al., 2017). Personalisation
23presenting a user with outputs derived from their own profile rather than population averages
significantly improves engagement (Aguilera et al., 2026).
These imperatives directly shaped the patient-facing profile of theHealthLink Kenya
dashboard. The light-themed interface ( #F7F9FC base, navy accent) was selected for read-
ability in high-ambient-light field conditions. The pin-drop facility locator restricts visible
outputs to facilities within a user-defined geographic grid. Asymmetric access controls
ensure thatPsees only their own data, preserving privacy while maintaining full analytical
depth for authorised outreach personnel.
The evidence reviewed above, however, applies primarily to patients who have already
initiated engagement with the health system. The conditions for effective self-navigation
identified by Valdivia et al. (2019) awareness of the tool, motivation to use it, and sufficient
health literacy to act on outputs are precisely the conditions that health-evasive populations
lack by definition. Applying self-navigation evidence uncritically to a health-evasive target
population would substantially overstate the patient portal’s reach. TheHealthLink Kenya
patient portal is therefore most accurately characterised as a retention tool for the recently
re-engaged, and a guided navigation aid for patients introduced to the system through
CHV-mediated encounters, rather than a standalone self-activation tool for the persistently
evasive.
2.5.4 Ethical Obligations in Predictive Outreach Systems
The deployment of predictive risk-scoring systems in community health outreach generates
three categories of ethical obligation that must be treated as design constraints rather than
post-hoc compliance measures. These obligations are introduced here because they directly
shaped theHealthLink Kenyadesign and because they are most legible when understood
against the evidence on health-evasive populations and CHV practice reviewed above.
The harm of misclassification.A predictive model operating in a triage context pro-
duces two categories of consequential error. False negatives households predicted low-risk
who are genuinely high-risk result in missed outreach and potentially preventable harm.
In a population already characterised by structural exclusion, this error compounds exist-
24ing disadvantage: the patients most likely to be misclassified are those whose profiles are
underrepresented in training data, including the rural and low-wealth households dispropor-
tionately absent from this study’s analytical dataset. False positives households predicted
high-risk who do not require priority intervention result in misallocated CHV time and, more
seriously, the risk of unsolicited contact that a health-evasive household may experience as
intrusive, potentially reinforcing avoidance behaviour rather than reversing it (Kraschenin
et al., 2008). The retention model’s operating characteristic under the SMOTE configuration
approximately four false positives for every true dropout identified must be disclosed to every
SWuser before deployment. Aggregate performance metrics such as AUC and F1-score do
not capture these real-world consequences.
Protection from risk-label stigma.A household assigned a “high-risk” classification
in a community outreach context carries that label into a social environment where it
may become visible to neighbours, community leaders, or family members. In Kenya’s
community health system, where CHVs are embedded members of the communities they
serve, the boundary between professional and social knowledge is porous. A risk score
that functions as a triage tool in a clinical context can function as a social label in a
community context, with consequences for dignity, standing, and the patient’s subsequent
willingness to engage with the health system (Cheruto, 2020).HealthLink Kenya’sdesign
response presenting risk outputs as care pathway guidance rather than risk classifications,
and restricting score visibility to authenticated accounts partially mitigates this risk. Full
mitigation requires community-level communication about how the system works and what
a CHV visit prompted by a model recommendation means.
Algorithmic accountability.When aSWprioritises one household over another because
a model recommended it, and the deprioritised household subsequently experiences a
preventable health crisis, the question of accountability who decided, on what basis, and
with what oversight becomes institutional rather than technical. This study addresses this
obligation through TreeSHAP transparency, open-source publication of the pipeline, and
the design principle that the model is a decision-support aid, not a decision-maker (Mackin
et al., 2025). These are necessary conditions for accountability, but not sufficient without a
25formal human override mechanism and a documented escalation pathway for cases where
SWjudgment conflicts with model output.
These three obligations translate into five specific design requirements, each documented
as an implementation requirement rather than an aspiration. First, no model output triggers
an automatic action; every recommendation requires human confirmation. Second, risk
scores are never presented toPas classifications the patient portal presents outputs as
personalised care pathway support, and the word “risk” does not appear in any patient-facing
module. Third, the operating characteristics of the retention classifier are disclosed to all
SWusers before deployment. Fourth, subgroup fairness analysis across gender, wealth
quintile, age group, and residential classification using demographic parity and equalised
odds metrics is identified as a pre-deployment requirement, not a future enhancement. Fifth,
a community communication protocol is required before anySWdeployment in a given
sub-county, grounded in the principle that communities subject to algorithmic prioritisation
have a right to understand the basis of that prioritisation (Masinde et al., 2025).
Taken together, the evidence in this section indicates that an effective response to health-
evasive populations requires a dual-track model: data-guided social worker outreach com-
bined with accessible patient-facing tools. TheHealthLink Kenyaframework operationalises
both tracks within a single deployment architecture, with the ethical responsibilities of each
track addressed through distinct but complementary design decisions.
2.6 Data Harmonisation, Governance, and Interoperability
2.6.1 The Interoperability Constraint in LMIC Health Systems
The scalability of any health analytics system is contingent on data interoperability: the
capacity to integrate heterogeneous datasets originating from different institutions, admin-
istrative levels, and collection instruments into a coherent analytical structure (Nyangena
et al., 2021). This constraint is not primarily technical; it is institutional. Prior research
in Kenya documents that heterogeneity in measurement protocols, inconsistent indicator
definitions, and variable data completeness routinely prevent the generalisation of analytical
26findings across geographic regions (Dijsselhof et al., 2024; Chepkirui et al., 2025). Uneven
digital literacy, fragmented institutional infrastructure, and limited technical capacity further
compound these barriers (Masinde et al., 2025).
Kenya’s health information system illustrates these constraints concretely. Nyangena
et al. (2021) assessed Kenya’s HIS interoperability readiness and found that while the
DHIS2 platform provides a national data exchange backbone, integration across facility-
level, administrative, and survey-based datasets remains incomplete due to inconsistent data
standards and governance gaps. Chepkirui et al. (2025) documented missed opportunities for
digital health data use in Homa Bay County, attributing them specifically to the absence of
analytical tools that translate collected data into operational decision support the precise gap
thatHealthLink Kenyaaddresses. The implications extend beyond infrastructure: where data
cannot flow consistently between levels of the health system,SWcannot make evidence-
informed targeting decisions, andPcannot be directed to the most appropriate facility.
2.6.2 Modular Pipelines as a Harmonisation Strategy
Modular data architectures in which preprocessing, transformation, and modelling stages are
implemented as independent, documented, and reusable components have been advanced as
a practical solution to interoperability failures in resource-constrained environments (Singh
et al., 2025; Alorbany et al., 2025). By decoupling data harmonisation from analytical
modelling, modular pipelines permit incremental updates and local customisation without
requiring system-wide redesign. This principle is consistent with the FAIR data framework
(Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable), which provides the open-access governance
scaffold for the pipeline published alongside this study (Billah et al., 2025).
The pipeline developed in this study implements this principle by organising preprocess-
ing into four independent modules each aligned with one of Andersen’s factor categories
before merging them into a single analytical dataset. Each module can be updated, replaced,
or extended independently, enabling the framework to accommodate new data sources or
expanded geographic scope without disrupting the downstream modelling architecture.
272.6.3 Deployment Architecture: Dashboard-Based Decision Support
The translation of predictive model outputs into practical decision-support tools for health sys-
tem administrators requires deployment architectures that are lightweight, low-connectivity-
tolerant, and interpretable for non-technical users. Streamlit-based interactive dashboards
have emerged as an accessible deployment mechanism, enabling real-time model inference
and interactive visualisation without requiring server-side infrastructure beyond a Python
runtime (Ray, 2025). Their single-process architecture makes them particularly well suited to
the resource constraints of LMIC deployment contexts, where server configuration expertise
may be absent and connectivity unreliable.
TheHealthLink Kenyadashboard adopts this architecture, making XGBoost predictions
and SHAP outputs accessible through dual interface profiles tailored toSWoutreach andP
self-navigation. The forward-compatible input schema aligned with HL7 FHIR R4 standards
provides a ready integration pathway with Kenya’s DHIS2 national health information
infrastructure (Byrne and Sæbø, 2022), positioning the framework as extensible rather than
standalone.
2.7 Synthesis: Specific Gaps Addressed by This Study
The reviewed literature establishes five specific methodological and operational gaps that
this thesis systematically addresses.
Gap 1: Andersen’s model is applied descriptively but not operationally.Across
LMIC applications, the model’s three factor categories are used to organise empirical findings
retrospectively (Solanke et al., 2023a; Orhan and Kurutkan, 2025) but are rarely translated
into structured feature engineering pipelines. This study operationalises Andersen’s cat-
egories as four independent preprocessing modules that directly govern feature selection,
imputation strategy, and encoding decisions.
Gap 2: Linear distance modelling suppresses policy-critical threshold effects.The
consistent application of linear distance specifications in LMIC health-seeking behaviour
models (Belay et al., 2025) masks the nonlinear inflection points at which utilisation collapses.
28This framework complements XGBoost classification with GAM-based distance modelling,
explicitly identifying threshold distances relevant to facility placement and mobile clinic
dispatch planning.
Gap 3: Class imbalance is treated as a modelling inconvenience rather than a
policy signal.Existing studies apply class-balancing techniques (Carvalho et al., 2025; Sani
et al., 2025) but do not systematically re-examine what the misclassified minority represents
in operational terms. This thesis reframes false positive predictions as a discrete target
population for differentiated policy intervention, with direct implications forSWoutreach
prioritisation.
Gap 4: Interpretable ML models are rarely deployed in African health information
systems.Despite documented advances in XAI for healthcare analytics (Yehuala, 2024; Awe
et al., 2025), their integration into deployable decision-support tools remains rare (Chepkirui
et al., 2025). TheHealthLink Kenyadashboard is designed to make TreeSHAP attribution
outputs directly accessible and actionable for bothSWadministrators andPusers.
Gap 5: FAIR-compliant, modular analytical pipelines for LMIC health-seeking
behaviour data are largely absent.The absence of openly documented, reproducible
harmonisation pipelines inhibits methodological transferability (Dijsselhof et al., 2024). The
complete preprocessing and modelling pipeline is published in an open-access repository
structured in compliance with FAIR principlesensuring that data and code areFindable,
Accessible,Interoperable, andReusable, thereby supporting transparency, reproducibility,
and cross-context applicability The ethical obligations generated by deploying predictive
risk tools in community settings misclassification harm, stigma risk, and algorithmic ac-
countability are treated throughout this study not as a sixth gap but as a cross-cutting design
constraint embedded in all five. As argued in Section 2.5, these obligations cannot be
addressed retrospectively; they must be resolved at the design stage.

4.1 Overview
This chapter presents the design of theHealthLink Kenyasystem, covering the analytical

descriptions.
No relational database is used in the current prototype. The system operates on serialised

4.2 System Architecture
HealthLink Kenyais implemented as a single-tier Streamlit application that integrates data

tecture was selected for three practical reasons: it eliminates inter-service communication
overhead, enables deployment on Streamlit Community Cloud without server configuration,

443.Inference Layer:The serialised XGBoost pipelines are loaded once and invoked on

4.Presentation Layer:Streamlit renders the analytical outputs predictions, SHAP vi-

4.3 Data Architecture and Persistence
HealthLink Kenyadoes not employ a persistent relational database in its current proto-
type form. Patient data submitted through the Predictive Triage module is processed
transiently within the inference pipeline and is not stored at any layer of the system.
The two serialised model artefacts, health_access_pipeline.pkl (785.22 KB) and

function as read-only inference resources loaded into memory at startup.
This design avoids data governance obligations under Kenya’s Data Protection

able patient records.

626 Discussion of Results
This chapter interprets the findings produced across the analytical and implementation stages
of the study, situating the quantitative outputs within the Andersen Behavioural Model
framework and the operational context of Kenyan healthcare planning. The analytical
findings are interpreted here in relation to three primary beneficiary groups:health-evasive
patientswho face structural or behavioural barriers to care-seeking;social workers and
community health workersresponsible for outreach and retention in Nakuru County; and
county health plannerswho require spatially disaggregated evidence for resource allocation.
TheHealthLink Kenyadashboard delivers asymmetric interfaces to each group: a light-
themed patient portal exposing only the individual’s own access probability, nearest facilities
within the 35 km grid, and retention score; and a dark-themed social worker console
providing population-level triage, geospatial risk overlays, and SHAP attribution outputs
scoped to Nakuru County.The discussion follows the sequence of the research objectives
stated in Chapter 1 and explains what the results mean, what they suggest or challenge
relative to prior literature, and why they matter for decision-making. No new results are
introduced here; all figures cited in this chapter are drawn directly from Chapter 4 and
Chapter 5.
6.1 Dataset Composition and Preprocessing Outcomes
The study commenced with 99,031 survey records from the 2022 KNBS Health-Seeking
Behaviour Survey. Following the synchronised cleaning protocol, 25,972 records (26.2%)
were removed due to missing distance_from_facility values, yielding a final analytical
dataset of 73,059 observations. This reduction is not a minor data quality step it is a
structural boundary on the study’s inferential scope, and must be interpreted as such. The
distance_from_facility variable could not be meaningfully imputed for these records because
missingness in this field typically reflects the absence of a named nearby facility altogether,
not a recording gap. Imputing a median or mean distance onto households that may have
63no proximate facility would introduce systematically false spatial signal into the model’s
most influential predictor. The listwise deletion was therefore methodologically necessary
given the study design. However, the missingness was not random. Analysis of the dropped
records confirms they were disproportionately associated with rural residential classifications
and lower wealth index quintiles. This pattern is consistent with the structural reality that
the populations least likely to identify a nearby facility remote rural households, informal
settlement residents, and the chronically under-documented are precisely those facing the
most acute access barriers. The consequence is significant: the working analytical dataset
represents healthcare access patterns among those already visible to the formal health
system, not among the most structurally excluded. All access probability estimates, SHAP
attributions, and GAM threshold findings in this study should be interpreted as lower bounds
on access failure in the Kenyan population, not as population-level truth. The model predicts
access among those who could report a facility; it cannot speak to those who could not.
The Winsorisation step, which capped distance_from_facility at the 5th and 95th
percentile bounds of 0.40 km and 35.00 km respectively, was a deliberate decision to reduce
the influence of extreme recording errors including a raw maximum value of 1,980 km that
exceeds Kenya’s geographic extent without discarding substantively valid observations. The
capping ceiling of 35km is consistent with the analytical distance threshold: the model
treats no patient as being further than 35km from a facility, while the GAM and SHAP
analyses jointly identify this distance band as the boundary of meaningful access.
6.2 Structural Determinants of Healthcare Access (RQ1)
The XGBoost TreeSHAP feature attribution analysis produced the study’s primary finding
with respect to RQ1. Contrary to the expected Andersen hierarchy in which enabling
factors (distance, wealth, insurance) were anticipated to dominatepredisposing factors
collectively accounted for 73.2% of the model’s predictive weight, with enabling factors
accounting for 26.8% (Li et al., 2023; Solanke et al., 2023b). Within the enabling category,
64distance_from_facility was the single most influential individual feature at 12.9% of
SHAP weight, with wealth index and insurance status contributing further weight.
This finding does not contradict the importance of physical distance as a structural barrier.
Distance is themost influential single variable. What the SHAP attribution reveals is that
the demographic composition of a patient their education level, age group, and working
status is collectively a stronger predictor of care-seeking than any individual structural
constraint. This suggests that health-seeking in the KNBS 2022 cohort is substantially
shaped by health literacy, life-stage demand (paediatric immunisation, occupational health
needs), and was consistent with care pathways, rather than by distance alone. The policy
implication is that narrowing the access gap requires both structural interventions (reducing
distance, extending insurance) and demand-side interventions (health education, community
health worker engagement).
The Cramér’s V categorical association analysis conducted during EDA independently
supported this finding, indicating strong associations between education level and work-
ing status, and between wealth index and insurance status, suggesting that enabling and
predisposing factors co-vary systematically in this population (Musiega et al., 2023).
6.3 Distance as a Structural Barrier and the GAM Analysis (RQ1)
Although predisposing factors dominate the aggregate SHAP attribution, distance remains
the single most important individual feature and the primary structural lever available
to health planners. The univariate GAM fitted on distance_from_facility against
visited_provider characterises the functional form of this relationship. As documented
in the notebook commentary, the GAM curve within the KNBS cohort remains relatively flat
across most distance bands a consequence of the positive selection bias inherent in facility-
based surveys: individuals who never engaged with the health system are structurally absent
from the analytical dataset. The curve shows a measurable decline in access probability
beyond the upper distance bands, consistent with the distance-decay hypothesis (Andersen,
2024).
65The multivariate XGBoost model, by controlling for all other features simultaneously,
isolates distance’s conditional effect more precisely than the univariate GAM. The SHAP
dependence plot for distance_from_facility suggests that shorter distances strongly
increase predicted access probability while longer distances drive it down a non-linear
relationship that linear classifiers cannot capture, as evidenced by Logistic Regression’s
substantially inferior AUC of 0.614 compared to XGBoost’s 0.8144 . The gap between
these two performance figures is not an artefact of algorithmic sophistication; it reflects the
non-linear, threshold-structured nature of the distance-access relationship that the GAM and
SHAP analyses describe jointly (Waqas et al., 2024).
On the validity of the distance signal: perceived versus geodesic distance.A concern
that arises directly from the GPS geocoding work described in Section 6.8 is whether
the SHAP finding that distance accounts for 12.9% of model weight represents a genuine
spatial barrier or a respondent perception artefact. A near-zero correlation (r = 0.040)
between self-reported and geodesic distance across 23,259 matched rows confirms that the
distance feature used in this model captures respondents’ perceptions of proximity, not
verified geographic distance. This materially limits the reliability of the spatial conclusions
drawn from SHAP and GAM analyses. Nonetheless, perceived distance is an independently
documented determinant of health-seeking behaviour in LMIC contexts (Belay et al., 2025;
Waqas et al., 2024) This finding does not invalidate the SHAP result it reinterprets it.
The model was trained and evaluated on the same self-reported measure throughout; its
internal consistency and predictive validity on held-out data (AUC 0.8144 ) are therefore
unaffected. What the low correlation was consistent with the model’s distance signal captures
thebehavioural and psychological experience of perceived distancethe effort, cost, and
deterrence that respondents associate with reaching care rather than the Euclidean gap
between two coordinate pairs. Perceived distance is itself an independently documented
determinant of health-seeking behaviour in LMIC contexts (Belay et al., 2025; Waqas et al.,
2024): what a patient believes about how far away a facility is influences their care-seeking
decision regardless of what a GPS device would record. The predictive signal is therefore
theoretically coherent even in the absence of geometric precision.
66What the near-zero correlation does reveal is that substituting geodesic distance for
self-reported distance would not simply improve measurement precision it would introduce a
structurally different predictor that the current model has never been trained on, and retraining
on the 23.5% of rows with geocoded coverage would be required to assess whether geometric
or perceived distance carries greater predictive weight for care-seeking in this population.
That comparison constitutes a distinct and valuable future research contribution, which the
GPS geocoding pipeline produced as part of this project has already positioned for execution
once household-level GPS coordinates become available in a subsequent KNBS survey
round.
6.4 Model Selection and Predictive Performance (RQ3)
The nine-algorithm tournament produced a clear and theoretically interpretable ranking.
The gradient boosting family XGBoost, AdaBoost, and Gradient Boosting systematically
outperformed linear classifiers and single decision trees across all evaluation metrics. The
final tuned XGBoost model achieved a held-out ROC-AUC of 0.8144 and F1-score of 0.8091
on the 73,059 cleaned observations, with bootstrap confidence intervals suggesting stability
(F195% CI:0.8035 –0.8139 ; AUC 95% CI:0.8069 –0.8216 ). The generalisation gap of
0.013 between cross-validated AUC ( 0.8015 ) and held-out test AUC ( 0.8144 ) suggests the
model generalises correctly to unseen data (Wiens et al., 2025).
The Ensemble (Top 3) achieved a marginal AUC improvement of only 0.0001 over
standalone XGBoost while introducing the architectural complexity of a V otingClassifier
and the instability of the GAM’s discriminative component (GAM tournament AUC: 0.532 ).
The principle of parsimony accordingly favoured the single XGBoost model for deployment
a decision consistent with the CRISP-DM framework’s emphasis on maintainability in
production environments (Martínez-Plumed et al., 2019).
Logistic Regression’s F1 of 0.661 and AUC of 0.614 in the tuned evaluation suggests
that the performance gap between linear and ensemble methods reflects the non-linear data-
generating process rather than algorithmic sophistication alone. The Brier score of 0.1760
67suggests that the XGBoost model’s probability outputs are well-calibrated for clinical
decision support.

6.5 Sensitivity Bias and the Class Imbalance Problem
The confusion matrix analysis revealed a pronounced sensitivity bias: the classifier achieved
near-perfect recall for the “Visited” majority class at the near-total expense of the “Not
Visited” minority class (Carvalho et al., 2025; Sani et al., 2025). This is not a model failure
in the conventional sense it is a diagnostic finding about the population being modelled. The
KNBS survey was administered at facilities and therefore already represents a positively
selected group: the unobserved population of individuals who never engaged with the health
system is structurally absent from the training data. The model has learned, correctly, that
the base-rate prediction for any individual in this dataset is “will visit.”
The practical implication is that the access probability scores rendered in the Predictive
Triage module should be interpreted as measures offacilitated access likelihoodconditional
68on an individual being in the survey population, not as unconditional population-level access
probability. This limitation is explicitly documented in the system’s FAQ module and is
acknowledged as a known property of survey-derived health access models.
The application of scale_pos_weight during hyperparameter tuning partially
addressed the Stage 1 imbalance; at the Stage 1 ratio of approximately 4.5:1,
scale_pos_weight outperformed SMOTE on F1-score ( 0.9034 versus 0.8374 ) and was
therefore selected as the preferred correction strategy for the access model. The baseline
Stage 2 model, with an original 5Stage 2 Retention Model faces a qualitatively more severe
imbalance of 28.74 :1, at which scale_pos_weight alone is insufficient to generate ade-
quate gradient signal from the 1,046 minority training cases. A SMOTE experiment applied
to the retention training partition suggested this: synthetic oversampling raised dropout recall
from 5.0% to53.4% identifying 140 of 262 true dropouts versus 13 at baseline at the cost of
a reduction in dropout precision from 0.867 to0.208 and a marginal AUC reduction from
0.8510 to0.8292 . The performance of the Stage 2 dropout model reflects a fundamental
trade-off between recall and precision. The SMOTE-enhanced model substantially improves
recall, enabling the identification of a greater proportion of at-risk patients. However, this
improvement comes at the cost of reduced precision, meaning that a significant proportion
of flagged cases may not actually result in dropout. From an operational perspective, this
implies that the model is better suited for broad risk screening rather than precise targeting.
In resource-constrained settings, this trade-off may have implications for workload alloca-
tion, as healthcare workers may need to engage with a larger number of patients to identify
true cases of risk. Accordingly, the model should be deployed with threshold tuning based
on local capacity, allowing stakeholders to balance sensitivity and efficiency depending on
available resources. (Carvalho et al., 2025; Sani et al., 2025).
A sensitivity analysis of the visited_provider target encoding further suggested that
the imputation assumption has negligible influence on model performance. Three encoding
strategies mapping “Don’t Know” to 0, to1, and dropping it entirely produced a maximum
AUC range of 0.0073 across identical pipelines, below the 0.01 robustness threshold. The
current encoding (“Don’t Know”→0) is retained as the most conservative choice.
69The retention model’s operating characteristics under the SMOTE configuration carry
a specific ethical implication that performance metrics alone do not surface. A precision
of 0.208 means that approximately four in five patients flagged as dropout-risk are false
positives. In a resource-constrained outreach context, each false positive represents a CHV
visit to a household that did not require priority intervention a cost measured in time,
transport, and the opportunity cost of a household that did need the visit and did not receive
it. More significantly, an unsolicited CHV visit to a health-evasive household, prompted by
a false positive flag, risks being experienced as institutional intrusion potentially reinforcing
avoidance behaviour in a population already defined by low system trust. The precision
trade-off is not a technical inconvenience; it is an ethical operating parameter that must be
disclosed to every CHV using the retention model, and must be considered in the design of
any community communication protocol preceding deployment.
6.6 The Urban Proximity Paradox
The most policy-significant finding from the error analysis is the Urban Proximity Paradox:
2,929 false positive cases in the test partition, representing individuals whose demographic,
socioeconomic, and geographic profiles predicted successful access but who did not visit
a healthcare provider. Projected across the full analytical dataset, this corresponds to
approximately 3,484 cases (Mwaura et al., 2025).
These individuals are not geographically isolated. Their failure to access care cannot be
attributed to physical distance alone. The profile of paradox cases clustering in urban and peri-
urban catchment areas suggests that access failure in these cases is driven by quality-of-care
barriers: long waiting times, perceived poor service quality, staff absenteeism, or stockout
rates. This distinction is policy-critical: geographic interventions (new facility construction,
mobile clinics, transportation subsidies) are the appropriate response to distance-driven
access failure, while quality-of-care interventions (clinical governance, community health
worker engagement, patient feedback systems) are required for proximity-paradox cases. A
facility catchment area with high paradox density requires a fundamentally different policy
70response than one with high distance-barrier density, even if both present with similarly
depressed access rate statistics. TheHealthLink Kenyapatient portal operationalises this
distinction directly. Health-evasive patients residing within the paradox zone geographically
proximate to facilities but not engaging with them are the dashboard’s primary end-user.
The pin-drop facility finder, constrained to the 35 km grid threshold provided by the GAM
analysis, surfaces the nearest NHIF-accredited facilities ranked by distance and service
type. For the Nakuru County pilot, this includes Nakuru Level 5 Referral Hospital (2.4 km),
Njoro Health Centre (9.1 km), and Gilgil District Hospital (18.7 km) the precise facilities a
peri-urban paradox patient is most likely to be directed to by a community health worker.
The social worker console complements this by flagging the 3,484 paradox cases as a distinct
triage category in the Outreach Hub, allowing social workers in Nakuru County to prioritise
quality-barrier outreach separately from distance-barrier outreach.

6.7 Stage 2: Retention Model and the observed Andersen Hierarchy Reversal
The Stage 2 Retention Model (AUC 0.8510 , F10.9836 ) reveals a theoretically significant
finding: while enabling factors dominate initial access at 26.8% of SHAP weight (with
distance as the largest individual predictor),predisposing factors dominate retention
at70.5% , with enabling factors accounting for only 27.2% and distance dropping to 4.9%
71(Li et al., 2023). This reversal suggests that the determinants of getting patients into the health
system differ structurally from the determinants of keeping them engaged. Infrastructure
investment addresses access failure; education, chronic disease literacy, and community
engagement programmes address retention failure. These are structurally different problems
requiring structurally different responses a finding that directly operationalises the two-stage
analytical framework promised in Chapter 1.
A post-hoc recall improvement analysis addressed the Stage 2 model’s primary oper-
ational limitation. Three strategies were evaluated on the held-out retention test partition:
threshold optimisation (T1), SMOTE oversampling (T2), and SMOTE combined with ag-
gressive scale_pos_weight (T3). T1, which lowers the classification boundary from 0.50
to the F1-optimal threshold of 0.95, raised dropout recall from 5.0% to16.4% with no
retraining and no change in AUC; this constitutes the minimum immediate fix deploy-
able on the existing serialised pipeline. T2 raised recall to 53.4% a ten-fold improvement
over baseline at the cost of a precision reduction from 0.867 to0.208 and an AUC of
0.829 . T3 (SMOTE with scale_pos_weight×3 ) produced worse recall than T2 ( 10.3% ),
suggesting that once SMOTE has balanced the training distribution, additional penalty
weighting is counterproductive. T2 is therefore the recommended production configuration;
theretention_pipeline.pkl artefact should be rebuilt using SMOTE prior to the next
dashboard deployment cycle.
6.8 Acknowledged Limitations
Five limitations bound the conclusions drawn in this study and must be considered in any
application of its findings.
Geographic selection bias and population scope.The 26.2% reduction in the analytical
sample due to missing distance_from_facility values was methodologically necessary:
missingness in this field typically reflects the complete absence of a named nearby facility,
not a recording gap, and imputing a median distance onto households with no proximate
facility would have introduced false spatial signal into the model’s most influential predictor.
72However, the missingness was not random. Dropped records were disproportionately associ-
ated with rural residential classifications and lower wealth index quintiles the same groups
identified in the literature as facing the most severe access barriers (Moturi et al., 2022; Belay
et al., 2025). The analytical dataset therefore skews toward households with documented
facility proximity, likely overstating population-level access rates and understating exclusion
risk for the most marginalised segments. All access probability estimates, SHAP attributions,
and GAM threshold findings should be interpreted as lower bounds on access failure in the
Kenyan population, not as population-level truth. The model predicts access among those
who could report a facility; it cannot speak to those who could not. Policy recommendations
particularly the 35 km GAM threshold and the SHAP-based barrier rankings should not be
generalised to populations outside the survey’s visible frame without GPS-linked census
data that can re-incorporate the dropped households into spatial analysis.
Positive selection in the GAM analysis.The univariate GAM curve reflects the distri-
bution of individuals who engaged with the health system. The absence of non-engaging
individuals from the dataset means the GAM cannot characterise the access probability of
the unobserved population. The threshold distances identified by the GAM represent the
point at whichengagedrespondents decline in access frequency, not the point at which
access failure begins for the full Kenyan population. The 35 km threshold should therefore
be treated as a behavioural heuristic grounded in respondent perception rather than a verified
geometric boundary.
Residual minority class recall and ethical operating characteristics.The baseline
Stage 2 retention model identified only 5.0% of true dropouts at the default classification
threshold, making it unsuitable for deployment in its baseline configuration. A post-hoc
SMOTE experiment raised dropout recall to 53.4% a ten-fold improvement and the rec-
ommended production configuration but 46.6% of true dropouts remain undetected even
with SMOTE, reflecting the structural constraint of the 28.74 :1class imbalance. The pre-
cision trade-off ( 20.8% dropout precision, approximately four false alarms for every true
dropout identified) carries a specific ethical implication beyond statistical inconvenience.
In a resource-constrained outreach context, each false positive represents a CHV visit to a
73household that did not require priority intervention a cost measured in time, transport, and
the opportunity cost of a household that did need the visit and did not receive it. More signif-
icantly, an unsolicited CHV visit to a health-evasive household, prompted by a false positive
flag, risks being experienced as institutional intrusion, potentially reinforcing avoidance
behaviour in a population already defined by low system trust. This operating characteristic
must be disclosed to every CHV user before deployment and should inform threshold tuning
based on local capacity. Stage 1 access probability scores remain conditional on survey
participation and cannot be generalised to the unobserved non-engaging population.
Operational adoption uncertainty and patient portal mismatch.The system valida-
tion reported in Chapter 5 is analytical and technical. It confirms that the dashboard correctly
implements the framework, that all functional tests pass, and that predictions are statistically
calibrated. It does not confirm that social workers, Community Health V olunteers, or county
health planners will adopt, trust, or sustain use of the tool in practice. Three structural
barriers create genuine adoption risk: Kenya’s CHV cadre is characterised by high turnover,
low or unpaid compensation, and limited time for activities beyond direct household contact;
digital literacy varies substantially within the CHV workforce; and adoption of new digital
tools in frontline health settings without explicit supervisory mandate and embedded training
has consistently failed to reach sustained use in comparable LMIC contexts (Chepkirui et al.,
2025; Lilan et al., 2025). These barriers do not invalidate the tool’s design; they specify the
implementation conditions under which it could succeed. Those conditions are not yet in
place.
A related mismatch concerns the patient-facing interface. The study defines health-
evasive patients as individuals characterised by low system trust, low digital literacy, and
active avoidance of formal health institutions (Section 2.5.1). The patient portal, however,
requires login, structured data entry, and interpretation of risk outputs interaction patterns
that presuppose the engagement, literacy, and trust that health-evasive patients lack by
definition. The resolution embedded in the system design that the patient portal functions
primarily as a retention and re-engagement aid used during CHV-mediated encounters, not
as a standalone evasion-reversal tool limits the portal’s independent reach to patients who
74have already crossed the first-contact threshold. This limitation constrains the scope of the
patient portal’s contribution: it supports retention, not initial engagement. Initial engagement
remains entirely dependent on the social worker console and the CHV outreach function it
supports.
Absence of a formal ethical governance framework.This study implements several
ethical design responses: TreeSHAP transparency, de-identified data, open-source publica-
tion, role-based access controls, human override logging (UC-03), and patient-facing output
sanitisation. These are necessary but not sufficient conditions for ethical deployment. Three
governance mechanisms required for county-level rollout are not yet in place.
The first is a pre-deployment subgroup fairness audit. The model’s predictions have
not been evaluated for systematic disparities across gender, wealth quintile, age group, or
residential area. A model that performs well on aggregate but disproportionately misclassifies
women, the rural poor, or specific ethnic communities would reproduce and potentially
amplify the structural exclusions it was designed to address. The TreeSHAP outputs provide
the analytical foundation for this audit; translating them into a formal demographic parity
assessment aligned with Kenya’s Data Protection Act (2019) governance standards is a
required pre-deployment step.
The second is a community consent and communication protocol. Households subject to
algorithmic prioritisation meaning households whose model-assigned risk score influenced
a CHV’s decision to visit have not been informed that such a system exists, what inputs it
uses, or what a model-prompted visit means. This is not solely a legal obligation under the
Data Protection Act (2019); it is an ethical obligation grounded in the right of communities
to understand the basis of decisions that affect them (Masinde et al., 2025).
The third is sustained bias monitoring across retraining cycles. A single pre-deployment
audit is insufficient if the model is retrained on new data. Each retraining cycle must repeat
the fairness evaluation to confirm that updated models do not introduce new disparities.
Without this, the governance framework degrades with each iteration.
Taken together, these five limitations define the boundary between what this study has
demonstrated and what remains to be established. The framework is analytically validated
75and technically implemented; its operational utility and ethical clearance for county-level
deployment are the primary objectives of the next phase of this project.
767 Conclusions, Recommendations and Future Work
7.1 Conclusions
The evidence accumulated across the analytical, modelling, and deployment stages of
this study suggests that a data-driven predictive framework grounded in the Andersen
Behavioural Model and trained on nationally representative survey microdata can provide
county health planners with actionable guidance for healthcare facility referral planning.
Kenya’s health information system has indicated readiness for data-driven tools at the county
level (Nyangena et al., 2021), and theHealthLink Kenyaframework is designed to meet that
infrastructure where it is rather than where it is not. The following conclusions are drawn
directly from the empirical work presented in this study.
Perceived physical distanceas reported by respondents is the dominant individual pre-
dictor of healthcare access in this dataset. Whether this reflects verified geographic barriers
or the accumulated deterrence of travel cost, road conditions, and prior experience requires
GPS-linked data to disentangle. Distance accounts for 12.9% of XGBoost predictive weight
more than any other single feature and its non-linear relationship with access probability
means that the marginal cost of additional distance accelerates beyond the upper distance
bands. This positions proximity to care as the highest-return infrastructure investment
available to county health planners, a finding directly actionable under Kenya’s Community
Health Policy framework.
the Andersen hierarchy appears empirically inverted who a patient is predicts
care-seeking more strongly than the structural barriers they face.Predisposing factors
collectively account for 73.2% of predictive weight in the access model. This finding does not
diminish the importance of structural enabling interventions; it encourages that demand-side
programmes health literacy, community health worker engagement, and targeted outreach
are equally necessary and are currently underweighted in facility planning decisions.
Initial access and retention are governed by structurally different determinant
hierarchies.The Andersen hierarchy reversal between Stage 1 (enabling factors individually
77important) and Stage 2 (predisposing factors dominant at 70.5% ) suggests that infrastructure
investment and education investment are complementary, non-substitutable policy responses.
This is the study’s most practically important finding for programme design, because it
means that a single intervention strategy cannot simultaneously address both access failure
and dropout risk.
The Urban Proximity Paradox requires quality-of-care interventions, not infrastruc-
ture expansion.The 3,484 paradox cases is consistent with a distinct, spatially identifiable
population experiencing access failure for reasons unrelated to geography. Their identifica-
tion through theHealthLink Kenyadashboard constitutes the study’s most directly actionable
policy contribution, as it allows social workers to direct quality-barrier outreach and facility
administrators to direct quality audits with spatial precision rather than relying on aggregate
utilisation statistics.
TheHealthLink Kenyaframework is analytically validated and operationally ready
for supervised field deployment.All four research objectives are met, the dashboard is
live, all twelve functional tests pass, and the core analytical outputs SHAP attributions,
GAM distance curves, and XGBoost probability scores are accessible to non-statistical users
through a plain-English interface. The framework is not a proof of concept; it is a working
system with two distinct validated interfaces. The patient portal empowers health-evasive
individuals to navigate to the right facility within the modelled 35 km access threshold
without requiring statistical literacy. The social worker console equips community health
workers with population-level retention intelligence, SHAP-grounded barrier attribution, and
a geospatial risk overlay scoped to Nakuru County the precise tools required for evidence-
based outreach prioritisation. The system awaits the structured operational validation that
will suggest its utility in practice. This finding must be read alongside the study’s most
important scope constraint: one in four survey respondents was excluded from the analytical
dataset because they could not identify a nearby facility suggesting the populations most
severely excluded by distance are structurally absent from the model’s training data.
787.2 Recommendations
7.2.1 For the Ministry of Health and National Health Planning Bodies
The GAM and SHAP distance analyses presented in this study provide an empirically derived
distance threshold that should be formally incorporated as a minimum-standard planning
metric for facility placement decisions under Kenya’s Community Health Policy framework.
Counties with high proportions of population residing beyond this modelled effective access
range should be prioritised for mobile clinic deployment, satellite dispensary construction,
or community health worker intensification. County planners should, however, treat the
35 km threshold as a behavioural heuristic grounded in respondent perception rather than a
verified geometric boundary; GPS-validated distance data from the Kenya Master Health
Facility List is recommended before any infrastructure deployment decision is made.
The Urban Proximity Paradox cases surfaced by theHealthLink KenyaGeospatial
Mapper provide a spatially precise basis for directing facility-level quality audits. Audit
instruments should assess waiting times, medicine stockout rates, staff attendance, and
patient-reported experience measures. Where Kenya’s health information system has already
begun integrating digital data flows at the county level (?), theHealthLink Kenyaframework
offers a ready analytical layer that can be connected to those existing data pipelines with
minimal architectural change.
No deployment ofHealthLink Kenyaat scale should proceed without the following
ethical preconditions being met, in order. First, a subgroup fairness audit must be completed
by a data scientist independent of the development team, evaluating model predictions
for demographic parity and equalised odds across gender, wealth quintile, age group, and
residential classification. Results must be reviewed by county health management before
any CHV is given access to triage outputs. Second, a community communication protocol
must be developed and delivered at the sub-county level before CHVs begin model-guided
outreach; community members should understand that CHV visits may be informed by a
predictive model, what data that model uses, and that a CHV visit is not a clinical judgment
about their health status. Third, the human override mechanism (UC-03) must be operational
79and CHVs trained in its use before triage access is granted; the log of override events should
be reviewed quarterly by county health information officers to identify systematic model
errors requiring retraining. Fourth, the retention model’s false positive rate approximately
four false flags for every true dropout identified must be communicated explicitly in CHV
onboarding; CHVs should be trained to use retention flags as prompts for further inquiry,
not as clinical verdicts. These are not bureaucratic requirements. They are the conditions
under which an analytically validated tool becomes an ethically deployable one.
7.2.2 For County Health Departments, Social Workers and Community Health Workers
County health offices considering deployment of theHealthLink Kenyaframework should
treat this as a phased implementation, not a direct rollout. Phase one before any CHV use
requires a structured onboarding programme not exceeding three hours, conducted at the
sub-county level, with hands-on navigation of the Predictive Triage and Geospatial Mapper
modules against real local scenarios. This programme should be led by a digitally literate
county health information officer, not by external researchers, to ensure it is sustainable
beyond the initial pilot. Supervisory endorsement meaning explicit instruction from sub-
county health management that dashboard outputs are to be used in caseload prioritisation
decisions is a precondition for sustained adoption, not an optional enhancement. Without
it, individual CHVs have no institutional reason to invest the time required to build tool
familiarity.
Phase two, once onboarding is complete, is where the operational recommendations apply.
Social workers in Nakuru County’s peri-urban fringes particularly Bahati, Njoro, Rongai,
and Subukia should use the Outreach Hub’s predictive triage table to prioritise household
visits by XGBoost risk score, filtering first by primary barrier type before planning outreach
routes. The Geospatial Mapper provides immediate visual identification of facilities in low-
access zones and paradox-flagged sites, enabling planners to build a spatial evidence base for
resource allocation requests without requiring statistical expertise. Facility administrators at
paradox-flagged sites should initiate community satisfaction surveys and cross-reference
results with XGBoost-derived retention probability scores; patients whose modelled retention
80probability falls below 60% should be flagged for proactive follow-up. The patient portal’s
pin-drop facility finder provides the corresponding patient-side tool, enabling re-engaged
patients to locate appropriate care within the 35 km grid without requiring social worker
intermediation. Phase two, however, is only reachable if phase one succeeds. This study has
produced the analytical foundation for phase one. It has not yet completed it.
7.2.3 For Health Insurance Providers and the NHIF
The SHAP analysis suggests that insurance status is among the most influential individual
enabling-factor predictors, and the uninsured population indicates systematically lower
access probabilities across all distance bands. Insurance providers should use the wealth
index quintile data and distance analysis produced by this framework to design targeted
enrolment campaigns in communities that combine geographic isolation with low insurance
penetration the population segment where a single enabling intervention yields the highest
marginal access improvement per programme shilling.
7.3 Future Work
Operationalising the SMOTE-based retention model improvement.The SMOTE ex-
periment indicated a ten-fold improvement in dropout recall from 5.0% to53.4% on the
held-out retention test partition. This result is computationally suggested; the next step is
operational deployment. The retention_pipeline.pkl artefact must be rebuilt using the
SMOTE procedure and redeployed to the Streamlit dashboard, after which the retention
module’s risk outputs will be substantially more useful for identifying patients at dropout
risk. The classification threshold should be exposed as a user-adjustable parameter, with
0.95 as the recommended default for planners prioritising identification of at-risk patients
over minimising false alarms. The residual gap 46.6% of true dropouts remain undetected
even with SMOTE points toward the supply-side feature work described below, which is
expected to provide the additional signal needed to close it further (Carvalho et al., 2025).
81Integration of high-resolution GPS coordinates.Replacing self-reported distance with
GPS-derived geodesic distances computed from verified facility coordinates would unlock
three capabilities currently unavailable: isochrone-based catchment delineation accounting
for road network topology; facility-level clustering analyses identifying service gaps at the
ward and sub-county level; and travel-time models that distinguish distance failure from
transport-cost failure (Belay et al., 2025). Kenya’s Master Health Facility List, maintained by
the Ministry of Health and available through the Kenya Health Information System (KHIS)
portal, provides verified GPS coordinates for all licensed public facilities and represents the
ready primary data source for this enhancement.
A geocoding pipeline was implemented and executed as part of this project, processing
the10,668 unique facility names recorded in the KNBS dataset via the Nominatim Open-
StreetMap API. The pipeline successfully geocoded 1,558 facilities ( 14.6% geocode rate),
with county locations inferred from Nominatim address strings for 1,504 of the geocoded
entries. Geodesic distances were computed from each geocoded facility’s verified GPS
coordinates to the centroid of its Nominatim-inferred county, yielding geodesic distance
estimates for 23,259 analytical rows (23.5% of the full dataset).
The resulting geodesic distance distribution exhibited a mean of 29.14 km, a median
of14.28 km, a standard deviation of 48.27 km, and a range of 0.21 km to 370.31 km
capturing the full geographic spread of Kenyan health facilities from dense urban clusters to
remote facilities in arid northern counties.
The Pearson correlation between self-reported distance and geodesic distance across the
23,259 matched rows was r=−0.040 a near-zero coefficient suggesting that respondents’
perceptions of distance to their facility bear no systematic linear relationship to the verified
geographic distance between their county centroid and that facility. This result empirically
substantiates the perception-bias limitation identified in Section 6.8 and provides direct
quantitative justification for the GPS integration priority: the current model’s distance feature
is perceptually rather than geometrically grounded, and replacing it with verified geodesic
measurements would introduce materially different and more analytically valid spatial signal
into both the XGBoost model and the GAM distance-decay analysis. The geocoded facility
82lookup table and enriched dataset ( knbs_with_geodesic_distance.csv ) are archived as
project outputs and are available for integration with a subsequent KNBS survey round that
geo-tags respondent households.
Incorporation of supply-side quality metrics to explain the Urban Proximity Para-
dox.The current feature set identifiesthataccess failure occurs among geographically
proximate patients with favourable profiles; it cannot yet explainwhy. Resolving this re-
quires supply-side variables that characterise what facilities offer rather than what patients
bring. Four specific indicators would materially improve explanatory power: medicine
stockout rates (available from the DHIS2 Harmonised Health Facility Assessment); clinician-
to-patient ratios (Kenya Health Workforce Observatory); average outpatient waiting times
(facility registers or patient exit surveys); and patient satisfaction scores (Kenya Patient
Satisfaction Survey, Ministry of Health).
A proof-of-concept integration scaffold was implemented to indicate the architectural
feasibility of this extension. A synthetic supply-side feature matrix was constructed from pub-
lished Kenyan facility quality distributions stockout rate ( β(2,5) , mean ≈0.29 ), clinician-
to-patient ratio ( Γ(2,0.6) ), average outpatient waiting time ( Γ(3,40) minutes), and patient
satisfaction score ( β(5,2) , mean ≈0.71 ) and appended to the existing patient-level feature
matrix ( n=73,111 ). An XGBoost classifier retrained on the augmented matrix produced
a marginal change in Urban Proximity Paradox case count ( +16 cases,−1.5% reduction),
suggesting the expected result: randomly assigned supply-side values carry no facility iden-
tity signal and therefore cannot replicate the explanatory power of genuine facility-level data.
The paradox is caused by specific facilities with specific quality deficits, not by supply-side
variance in the abstract. This result strengthens the case for real DHIS2 data integration:
when supply-side values are joined on facility identifier rather than assigned randomly, the
XGBoost architecture requires no modification and is expected to produce material paradox
reductions. Incorporating these variables will require a multi-level modelling architecture
separating facility-level from patient-level predictors a substantive extension that constitutes
a distinct future research contribution.
83Formal usability trials with health facility administrators.TheHealthLink Kenya
dashboard is analytically validated and publicly accessible; the critical remaining evidence
is whether county health planners, health-evasive patients and social workers and facility
administrators find it useful in practice. A structured usability trial involving health-evasive
patients recruited through community health worker networks and social workers from
at least two contrasting sub-counties within Nakuru County would provide this evidence.
Outcome measures should include System Usability Scale (SUS) scores, task completion
rates for the pin-drop facility finder (patient cohort) and the Predictive Triage and Geospatial
Mapper modules (social worker cohort) , and qualitative interview data on terminology
clarity, clinical relevance, and adoption barriers. Findings would directly inform dashboard
iteration before any Ministry of Health endorsement is sought. Experience from analogous
data-driven tools in Kenyan health settings suggests that uptake is achievable when tools
are lightweight, locally adapted, and designed with end-user workflows in mind (Chepkirui
et al., 2025; Lilan et al., 2025).
Longitudinal survey linkage and persistent patient tracking.The cross-sectional
design of the KNBS 2022 instrument prevents tracking of individual health-seeking trajec-
tories over time. Two complementary extensions would address this. At the survey level,
probabilistic record linkage between successive KNBS rounds using anonymised household
identifiers would enable a panel dataset for training the retention model on genuine temporal
sequences rather than a static revisit indicator. At the system level, a production deploy-
ment would require a normalised relational database anchoring on Patient ,Visit , and
Prediction entities as described in Section 4.3, enabling longitudinal monitoring and early
detection of retention failure.
Formal ethical AI audit and governance certification.A structured fairness evaluation
assessing whether access and retention probability predictions systematically disadvantage
specific gender, age, wealth, or residential subgroups using demographic parity, equalised
odds, and calibration-by-subgroup metrics should be completed before any county-level
rollout. Beyond the technical audit, a governance certification process aligned with Kenya’s
Data Protection Act (2019) and the emerging African Union Data Policy Framework should
84be initiated, producing a documented ethical clearance that county health offices can present
to community stakeholders as evidence that the system meets established human rights and
data ethics standards. This certification is not a one-time step; it should be repeated with
each model retraining cycle.
A probabilistic record linkage scaffold was implemented to indicate the feasibility of
the survey-level extension. Simulating two successive survey waves by stratified sampling
of the KNBS 2022 dataset ( n=2,995 per wave), blocking on age group and gender, and
applying exact-match comparison on education level produced 139,938 matched pairs at a
match rate of 27.99% . The resulting pseudo-panel dataset the structural form a genuine two-
wave panel would take yielded the following care-seeking trajectory distribution: Retained
(66.4% ), Dropped out ( 15.4% ), Re-engaged ( 14.6% ), and Never engaged ( 3.7% ). This
four-class trajectory outcome is the target variable the retention model should be trained on
when real longitudinal data exists, replacing the current static binary revisited_provider
indicator. The two extensions are structurally independent; the database architecture can be
implemented in the next deployment iteration without waiting for a new survey round.
Live integration with Kenya’s DHIS2 national health information system for real-
time referral support.The current implementation uses static facility metrics. Connecting
theHealthLink Kenyainference layer to the DHIS2 API would replace these with live data
on facility utilisation, stock levels, and service availability, ensuring referral recommenda-
tions reflect current capacity rather than survey-period estimates. The technical pathway is
already partially implemented: the dashboard’s input schema is structurally compatible with
HL7 FHIR R4 Patient andObservation resource schemas, which are the standard inter-
change format between DHIS2 and Kenya’s national Digital Health Infrastructure (Nyangena
et al., 2021). Full integration would require an authenticated DHIS2 API connection, a
scheduled data refresh pipeline, and formal data-sharing agreements with county health
management information system officers steps that are technically straightforward given the
existing architectural compatibility.
Geographic expansion to all 47 counties.The current Geospatial Mapper is calibrated
to Nakuru County facilities. Fitting county-specific GAM distance curves on subnational
85KNBS survey strata and extending the facility dataset to all 47counties would produce a tool
capable of directly informing Kenya’s Universal Health Coverage roadmap at national scale.
The modular pipeline architecture was deliberately designed to support this geographic
scaling without requiring structural re-engineering, meaning the primary constraint is data
availability rather than system capacity.
USSD and SMS channel extension for low-connectivity peri-urban users.The
currentHealthLink Kenyapatient portal requires a smartphone and data connection a barrier
that mirrors the very access problem the system is designed to address. Nakuru County’s
peri-urban fringes, where the Urban Proximity Paradox density is highest, also exhibit
lower smartphone penetration rates relative to the urban core. A USSD interface exposing
the facility finder and retention score via short-code session (e.g. *384# ) would extend
the system’s reach to feature-phone users without requiring any change to the underlying
XGBoost inference layer. An SMS reminder module, triggered by the retention model’s
dropout flag, would complement the social worker outreach function by reaching at-risk
patients directly between visits. Both extensions are architecturally feasible given the existing
API-compatible inference layer and represent the highest-priority platform expansion for the
next development cycle.
Ethical AI governance and formal bias audit.A structured fairness evaluation as-
sessing whether access probability predictions systematically disadvantage specific gender,
age, or wealth groups using demographic parity, equalised odds, and calibration by sub-
group should be completed before county-level rollout. The TreeSHAP attribution outputs
provide the analytical foundation for this audit; translating them into a formal assessment
against Kenya’s Data Protection Act (2019) governance standards is the final pre-deployment
governance requirement (Masinde et al., 2025).
